Lesson 1

What is statistical process control?

Statistical process control (SPC) breaks down into three ideas. Statistical: we work from data, not hunches. Process: engineers use that data to study how a process behaves. Control: the goal is to make the process behave the way we want, so that it reliably gives the output we need.

A process is a sequence of steps that turns inputs into a required output. Take growing a gate oxide in a furnace (dry oxidation in the diffusion module):

  • Inputs: 130 sccm of N2 flow, 60 sccm of O2 flow, 1000 °C, for 100 minutes.
  • Process: dry oxidation in the furnace.
  • Output: a 250 Å gate oxide that passes electrical test (ET) and reliability checks.

The key point: if the inputs or the process itself vary, the output will drift away from what we want. SPC is about spotting and managing that variation.

Lesson 2

Sources of variation: the 6M

Variation can creep in from many places. A handy way to remember them is the 6M: six sources that all start with M.

  • Man means the people. For example, an employee doesn't follow the standard operating procedure during preventive maintenance.
  • Machine means the equipment. For example, components wear out and fail over time.
  • Material means what goes in. For example, a new vendor supplies the gas, chemicals or wafer substrates.
  • Method means how the work is done. For example, the order of steps in a recipe or process flow changes.
  • Measurement means how we check results. For example, a different type of metrology tool is used to measure critical dimension (CD).
  • Mother Nature means the environment. For example, the particle count or relative humidity in the cleanroom changes.

When a result looks wrong, running through the 6M is a quick way to list the places the problem might have come from.

Lesson 3

Process variation vs measurement variation

The spread we see in our data comes from two places: the process really varying, and our measurements of it varying. Using variance (σ², the square of the standard deviation), they add up:

σ²total = σ²process + σ²measurement

  • σ²total is all the variation we see in the system.
  • σ²process is real variation in the product, from five of the 6M: Man, Machine, Material, Method and Mother Nature.
  • σ²measurement is variation added by the metrology system: equipment variation (EV) from the tool itself, and appraiser variation (AV) from the people using it.

If the measurement variation is large, it hides what the process is really doing. That's why keeping measurement variation small is key. Before a tool is used to measure critical parameters, the metrology team runs a gage repeatability and reproducibility (GR&R) study. It checks that the tool's variation stays within an acceptable limit and that the tool is accurate and reliable.

Lesson 4

Common and assignable causes of variation

Common (natural) causes

Every process has some natural variability built in. It comes from many small, random causes that are unknown and essentially unavoidable, adding up together. These causes are always present and don't lead to bad results. A process with only common causes of variation is said to be in statistical control.

Assignable (special) causes

These are not random. They can be traced to something specific, such as a change of material vendor, a tool used past its replacement point, or variation in incoming material. They aren't present all the time, but when they are, they often cause bad results such as lower yield or scrapped wafers. A process with assignable causes present is said to be out of control.

Lesson 5

The purpose of SPC

SPC does two jobs. It monitors how stable a process is over time. It also lets engineers step in correctly, and with confidence, when an assignable cause turns up. It does this using control charts and statistics.

Picture two charts of the same measurement over time. In the first, the values bounce up and down around 37.0 but stay in a narrow band (about 36.9 to 37.1). That's common-cause variation, and nothing needs fixing. In the second, the values bounce around 37.0 for a while, then suddenly jump and stay around 37.5 to 37.7. That sudden shift is a sign of an assignable cause, and it's exactly what SPC is meant to catch.

Lesson 6

Control chart basics

A control chart is a way to monitor a process while it runs. It's built to quickly show when the process goes out of control (OOC) because of an assignable cause.

Most processes follow a normal distribution, the bell-shaped curve. Most values sit near the centre, and they become rarer the further you move from it. In a normal distribution, about 68.26% of values fall within 1σ of the mean, 95.44% within 2σ, and 99.73% within 3σ.

A typical control chart has three lines:

  • The centre line is the mean (μ) of the measurement when the process is in control.
  • The upper control limit (UCL) is usually set at μ + 3σ, where σ is the standard deviation.
  • The lower control limit (LCL) is usually set at μ − 3σ.

With limits at ±3σ, 99.73% of samples from an in-control process fall between them. A sample outside the UCL or LCL is taken as evidence that the process is out of control.

For example, say the mean via diameter is 74 with σ = 0.0045. Then the UCL is 74 + 3 × 0.0045 = 74.0135 and the LCL is 74 − 3 × 0.0045 = 73.9865.

Lesson 7

Patterns in control charts

A point beyond the control limits isn't the only warning sign. A series of points that isn't random can also signal an out-of-control situation, even when every point is inside the limits.

  • Trend pattern: the points climb (or fall) steadily over time. Several points in a row on the same side of the centre line is called a run, and runs often show up in trends. A run of several samples is very unlikely if the data were truly random, so it can be treated as OOC.
  • Cyclic pattern: the points rise and fall in a regular wave. All of them may stay inside the limits, but the rhythm suggests something periodic, such as operator fatigue or raw material running low. Treat it as a possible OOC situation.

Lesson 8

Spotting OOC with the Western Electric rules

To catch non-random patterns like trends and cycles in a consistent way, engineers use the Western Electric (WE) rules. They split the chart into zones on each side of the centre line: zone C is within 1σ, zone B is between 1σ and 2σ, and zone A is between 2σ and 3σ.

A process is out of control if any of these happens:

  1. Any single point falls beyond the 3σ control limits.
  2. Two out of three points in a row fall beyond the 2σ warning limits (in zone A or beyond).
  3. Four out of five points in a row fall beyond 1σ (in zone B or beyond).
  4. Nine points in a row fall on the same side of the centre line.
  5. Six points in a row keep increasing, or keep decreasing.
  6. Fourteen points in a row alternate up and down.
  7. Fifteen points in a row fall in zone C, on either side of the centre line.
  8. Eight points in a row fall on both sides of the centre line, with none in zone C.

Each rule describes something that rarely happens by chance, so seeing one is a strong hint of OOC behaviour. Breaking any rule means a special cause is present in the process or equipment. That cause then has to be found and removed.

Source: G. S. May & C. J. Spanos, Semiconductor Manufacturing and Process Control, Ch. 6.

Lesson 9

Type I and Type II risks

Every time we read a control chart we make a decision: act, or leave the process alone. There are two ways to get that decision wrong.

Type I risk (alpha, α): the producer's risk

This is crying wolf when there's no wolf, like convicting an innocent defendant. In SPC terms, it means stepping in when the process is actually fine. The cost is wasted investigation, and sometimes scrapped wafers and lost revenue.

Type II risk (beta, β): the consumer's risk

This is failing to see the wolf that's really there, like letting a guilty defendant go free. In SPC terms, it means not stepping in when we should have. The cost is that defective wafers get shipped to the customer.

Decisions and their outcomes
Decision No real problem Real problem
Do nothing Correct decision Bad wafers shipped (Type II, β)
Do something Lost revenue (Type I, α) Correct decision

Lesson 10

Alpha risk and the 3σ limits

For a measurement that follows a normal distribution, 99.73% of points should fall randomly between the ±3σ control limits. Turn that around: about 0.27% of points will land outside the limits even when there's no special cause.

That 0.27% is the alpha (Type I) risk of a standard 3σ control chart. Roughly 1 in every 370 points will raise a false alarm, purely by chance.

Lesson 11

How the control limits set your risk

Where you put the control limits decides how much of each risk you take on:

  • Limits too wide: real drifts in the process stay inside the limits and go unnoticed. That means high beta risk.
  • Limits too tight: normal, stable variation keeps crossing the limits, causing lots of unnecessary OOC alarms. That means high alpha risk.
  • Limits off-centre: if the limits are shifted relative to the process, the side closer to the data raises too many false alarms (high alpha risk). The side further away misses real problems (high beta risk).

Lesson 12

Average run length

An important measure of any control chart is its average run length (ARL): the average number of samples taken before a point goes beyond the control limits.

When the process is in control:

ARL = 1 / α

where α is the probability of a Type I error. A high ARL here is good, because it means false alarms are rare. With 3σ limits, α = 0.0027, so ARL = 1 / 0.0027 ≈ 370 samples.

When the process is out of control:

ARL = 1 / (1 − β)

where β is the probability of a Type II error. A low ARL here is good, because it means the chart catches the problem quickly.
